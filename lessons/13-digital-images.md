# Photos, screenshots and image files

Pictures are everywhere on a computer: a profile photo, a scanned document, a diagram in a lesson, a screenshot of an error message. Knowing a little about image files saves time, space and confusion.

## The three formats you will meet

- **JPG (or JPEG)** — best for photographs. It makes files small but loses a little detail each time you save.
- **PNG** — best for screenshots, logos and images with sharp edges. It keeps every pixel but can be large.
- **SVG** — a drawing made of shapes and maths. It stays sharp at any size and is perfect for icons and diagrams.
- **GIF** — supports simple animation and a small number of colours.

> If you are sending a photo, JPG is usually fine. If you are sending a screenshot with text, use PNG.

## Pixels and resolution

An image is made of tiny squares called **pixels**. A photo that is 1920 by 1080 pixels is sharp on a laptop but may look soft on a large television. A picture that is 200 pixels wide will look blurry if you stretch it.

You can make a large image smaller without much harm. Making a small image larger adds invented pixels and looks fuzzy. Always start with the largest version you have.

## Cropping, resizing and compressing

- **Crop** removes the edges. Use it to put the subject in the middle.
- **Resize** changes the number of pixels. Keep the **aspect ratio** locked so faces do not look stretched.
- **Compress** reduces file size. Do this once, at the end, and keep the original.

Phones and computers have simple built-in editors. On Windows, open a photo in **Photos** and choose Edit. On a phone, use the edit button in the gallery.

## Screenshots

A screenshot copies what is on the screen.

- Windows: [kbd:Windows] + [kbd:Shift] + [kbd:S] opens the snipping tool.
- Mac: [kbd:Shift] + [kbd:Command] + [kbd:4] selects an area.
- Phones: usually a power button plus volume down.

Screenshots are perfect for showing a teacher an error message. Crop out private information first.

## Alt text and accessibility

Every image on a website or document should have **alt text**. It describes the image for someone using a screen reader, and it appears if the image fails to load.

Good alt text is short and useful: "Chart showing exam passes rising from 60% to 87%". Bad alt text is "image" or "picture1".

## Backing up and naming

Give files names you can understand: `school-id-front.png` is better than `IMG_2043.png`. Keep originals in a folder and copies in cloud storage. If your phone is lost, the photos are still safe.

## Privacy check

Before you post or send a photo, look at the background. Is there a house number, a school badge, a bank card or another person who has not agreed? Once a photo is online, you cannot really take it back.

## Try it

Find an image on your device. Crop it to a square, save a copy as JPG, and write one sentence of alt text for it.
